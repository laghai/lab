Your actual question, answered

What building a website consists of

Seven stages, in order, because the order genuinely matters — each one is expensive to redo once the next has started. The vocabulary in each is what a vendor or developer will use with you.

  1. Audience & content map

    Yours — about a week — no developer involved

    Decide who the site talks to and what each of them needs on arrival. You have already done most of this: donors, corporate and foundation partners, artists, educators and regulators. What's missing is the ruthless part — what each one needs to see first, and what gets cut. This stage decides whether the site works. Everything after it is execution.

  2. Information architecture

    Yours to approve — the nav, the page list, the URLs

    Turning the map into a structure: what pages exist, what the menu says, what lives under what. This is where 98 links becomes five. Ask to see it as a plain list before anyone designs anything.

  3. Wireframes

    Yours to approve — grey boxes, deliberately ugly

    Layouts with no color, no photography, no fonts — just what goes where and in what order. They look unfinished on purpose, so the conversation stays on priority instead of taste. This is the cheapest possible place to be wrong. Insist on it; vendors often skip it and it is the skip that causes expensive redesigns later.

  4. Visual design

    Designer — you and the founder approve

    The look: typography, color, photography treatment, how a mural photograph is cropped and used. This is where the charity:water reference actually applies, and it is the only stage where "make it beautiful" is the right note.

  5. Build

    Developer — you set the constraints, not the code

    Turning designs into a working site. Your Squarespace plan permits custom code and CSS, so a great deal is possible without leaving it. The question you'll be asked is "custom or template" — and the real question underneath is who maintains it after launch. See D2.

  6. Content production

    Yours — and it is the usual reason launches slip

    Photography, written copy, the founder's blog posts, project descriptions, impact numbers. Design gets blamed for delays; it is almost always content. Start this at stage 3, not stage 6.

  7. Launch & measurement

    Developer — but one item is yours to insist on

    Analytics, donation tracking, and redirects. When page addresses change, every old link — from Google, from press coverage, from years of newsletters — breaks unless each one is pointed at its replacement. A rebuild without a redirect plan will destroy the organic search you asked for. Ask for the redirect map in writing before launch day.

Three calls only you can make

Decisions

D1

Full overhaul, phased rollout, or just campaign landing pages?

  • One single overhaul — slowest to any result, and the whole budget is spent before you learn anything
  • Phased — but starting with the donation path, not the homepage
  • Landing pages only — traffic still funnels into the same broken PayPal hand-off
Recommendation Phase one is the giving experience: the eight tiers you already wrote, turned into actual buttons, on your own site, with recurring giving and a way to capture the donor's details. It is the smallest change on the list, the only one that touches the $300K directly, and it is reversible. Your instinct that the whole site needs redesigning is correct — but doing the donation path first means the redesign gets funded by a mechanism that already works, instead of the other way round.
D2

Stay on Squarespace, or rebuild custom?

  • Stay — treat Squarespace as the shell, add custom code where it earns its place
  • Rebuild custom — more control, and a permanent dependency on whoever wrote it
Recommendation The binding constraint is not what the code can do — it is who keeps the site alive after the developer's invoice is paid. A custom site with nobody in-house to maintain it decays visibly within a year, and for a nonprofit that decay reads to donors as instability. Squarespace is the unglamorous correct answer here. Spend the freed budget on photography and on the donation mechanism instead. One exception, and it is independent of everything else: move giving off the generic PayPal button and onto a real nonprofit donation platform. That change is worth making this month whatever you decide about the redesign.
D3

Who builds it — agency, freelancer, or you with tooling?

  • Agency — highest cost, and you'll spend much of it on account management
  • Freelancer or small studio, with you owning stages 1–3 yourself
  • You alone — the learning is real, but the calendar is 2–3 months
Recommendation This is the decision your original question was really about. If you personally own the audience map, the architecture and the wireframes — the three stages that don't require code — then you are briefing from a position of authority and any competent freelancer can execute it. That is the cheapest version, the fastest version, and the one where you genuinely learn the thing you said you wanted to learn. Three questions that separate a good vendor from a bad one: Will you show me wireframes before design? What is your redirect plan? Who updates this in six months?
